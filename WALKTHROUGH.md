## 7. Control room · Live session (Unit 1)

Into the RL control room. **Live session** is Unit 1 made concrete.

You set the starting state yourself: fatigue, tilt and streak. These three buckets *are* the state.

Streak: LOSING. A rough spot to be in.

The policy dropdown picks who decides: here the DP-optimal policy.

The seed fixes the random numbers, so this session is reproducible.

**Play one match**, one step of the loop.

The tape records the step: state, the action the policy chose, the reward, and the state it moved to. The gauges move with it.

**Auto-play** runs the rest of the session to a terminal state: a rage quit, a boredom quit, or simple exhaustion.

When the episode ends, the return is computed **backwards** from the last reward: Gt = Rt+1 + γ Gt+1. That is the definition, applied to the episode you just watched.

The curve plots G-nought for this same episode against γ, so you can see what discounting does.

Drag γ down to 0.2 and the agent becomes myopic: only the next match or two counts.

Push it to 0.99 and the whole session counts, which is why a far-sighted policy avoids rage quits.

**New seed** gives a different episode; **Reset** returns to the chosen start state.

## 8. Control room · Q-learning and the TD update

**Q-learning**. This is the screen that was mislabelled a DQN in version 3.

It is a **table**: 49 states by 3 actions. With 48 discrete states there is nothing to approximate, so no neural network is involved — and the app now says so everywhere.

The top map is the production policy: greedy with respect to the stored Q-table.

Below it, the **temporal-difference explainer**. Pick a state, an action, a step size and a seed.

The state is chosen by clicking it on the map — here a TIRED, TILTED, LOSING player.

Step size α of 0.1, so the move is visible.

**Sample a transition and update**.

It samples one real transition and shows the arithmetic: the reward, the bootstrapped target R + γ max Q(S',·), the TD error, and the new value.

The v3 bug lived right here. It bootstrapped from the next state even after a rage quit ended the episode. After a terminal step the target is just the reward, and that is what runs now.

The second tab is the **training lab**.

Every hyperparameter is exposed: episodes, α, γ, the ε schedule from 1.0 down to 0.05, the seed, and a state to watch while it learns.

I will train 8,000 episodes to keep this short. The shipped table used 30,000.

This checkbox is off by default and matters: training here does **not** overwrite the production policy unless you explicitly ask it to.

The curves are from the run that just happened: the return per episode, the ε schedule decaying, and the Q-values of the watched state settling.

The map at the end is the policy this run learned, with the states where it disagrees with the production table marked.

## 9. Control room · MDP explorer (Unit 3)

**MDP explorer**: Unit 3, the formal model.

The state space is fatigue 4, tilt 4, streak 3 — 48 states, plus an absorbing END state, with s = 12f + 3t + k.

The transition probabilities are **not** read off the simulator's formulas. They are estimated by rolling the simulator out 1,000 times per state-action pair: 144,000 simulated transitions, labelled a *model estimate*.

That sample count is a control, so you can see the estimate get sharper as it grows.

Pick the policy to inspect.

Click any state to open it.

Now the Bellman backup for that state, term by term, with real numbers: every next state, its estimated probability, the reward, and the discounted value it contributes.

The sum of that column *is* Q(s,a). Take the max over actions and you have the optimality equation, not as a formula but as arithmetic you can check.

Switch the action to RAISE and the whole breakdown recomputes.

The graph underneath draws where that action actually sends you, ranked by probability.

Fixing a policy collapses the MDP into a **Markov chain**, and the reward into an MRP. The app solves it in closed form, v = (I − γP)⁻¹R, and agrees with iterative evaluation to about 1e-8.

Last, the **model quality** panel: how far the estimated probabilities sit from the simulator's exact law. We can only measure that because we wrote the simulator.

## 10. Control room · Dynamic programming (Unit 4)

**Dynamic programming**: Unit 4, four tabs.

All four need the model, which is what makes them model-based. They run in milliseconds on 48 states.

First, **policy evaluation**.

Run it: sweep the Bellman expectation backup until the largest change falls below θ.

It reports the sweeps, the final residual, and the value map for the policy you evaluated. The closed-form solution is shown beside it as a cross-check.

Second, **policy iteration**.

Evaluate, improve, repeat.

Three improvement steps and it is policy-stable. The states that changed on each pass are outlined, so you can watch the policy actually move.

Improvement never lowers the value of any state. That is the policy improvement theorem, and it is asserted in the test suite.

Third, **value iteration**.

One optimality backup per sweep.

Forty-two sweeps to a tolerance of 1e-6 at γ = 0.9 — the experiments table quotes 52 for the same γ because it uses a tighter 1e-8. The interesting column is the ratio of successive changes: it never exceeds γ.

That is the contraction property — the Banach fixed-point argument — measured rather than asserted. The sequence is Cauchy, so it converges to a unique fixed point.

It also compares its answer with policy iteration. Same policy, different route.

#dTabs button:has-text("γ sweep")

Solve the MDP across a range of discount factors.

This is the clearest result in the project: as γ rises, the optimal policy lowers difficulty more often and the rage-quit rate falls. Patience is worth something, and here is how much.

## 11. RL lab · Bandit arena (Unit 2)

Into the RL lab. **Bandit arena**: Unit 2.

The arms are maps. Their true values come from the tournament fit score rescaled, which is a rule — so the arm means are a *derived feature* and the rewards are *simulated*.

You can also type arm means directly, which is how the lecture examples are reproduced.

The slider sets how many arms are in play.

Then the algorithms, each a checkbox: explore-only, greedy, explore-then-greedy, ε-greedy, optimistic initial values, and UCB.

c for UCB, the exploration weight in c√(ln t / N).

And the optimistic starting value: make every arm look good until it has been tried.

Averaged over 50 runs, all algorithms on identical seeded environments.

Rewards can be Bernoulli thumbs-up or Gaussian quality.

And this is the interesting one: a **balance patch** permutes the arm values halfway through.

Regret over time, and the share of pulls that were optimal. Greedy locks onto a mediocre map early. After the patch, the sample-average methods are stuck with their history.

That is the argument for a **constant step size**: it weights recent rewards exponentially, so it forgets the pre-patch world.

Set α to 0.1 and re-run to see that.

The inspector below opens one algorithm and replays its decisions.

And step through the rounds.

For each round it shows the counts, the mean estimates, the confidence term, and which arm the rule therefore picks — the whole UCB formula, instantiated.

Regret is reported two ways: the expected regret against the best arm, and the realised regret the lecture notes define.

## 12. RL lab · Monte Carlo lab (Unit 5)

**Monte Carlo lab**: Unit 5, three tabs.

Monte Carlo needs no model at all. It averages complete-episode returns, which is why every episode here has to terminate — and ours always does, because fatigue only rises.

Prediction first: choose the policy to evaluate.

Starting states spread uniformly so every state gets visited.

Two estimates side by side: **first-visit** uses the first time a state appears in an episode, **every-visit** uses all of them.

And the error against the DP answer as episodes accumulate. It falls roughly as one over the square root of the visits — experience buys accuracy, slowly.

Second tab: **control**, learning a policy rather than evaluating one.

It opens on **on-policy ε-soft**, which keeps exploring forever — so it converges to the best ε-soft policy, not the greedy optimum. That is why it lands near 97% rather than 100%.

**Exploring starts** is legitimate here because our simulator can be placed in any state, which is rare and worth saying out loud.

Every episode begins from a random state-action pair, so coverage is free and this variant gets closest to the optimum.

**Off-policy with weighted importance sampling** learns a greedy target from an exploring behaviour policy.

It only learns from the tail of each episode, after the last non-greedy action — which is why it trails the other two.

Third tab: **off-policy evaluation**, the practical question.

"Would a new matchmaker be better?" — answered from sessions logged under the current one, before deploying anything.

The ratio ρ is the product of π over b along the episode. **Ordinary** importance sampling is unbiased but high-variance; **weighted** is biased but stable; per-decision and discounting-aware cut the variance further.

Scenario one: a **small change** from the logged policy.

Estimate the target value.

All four estimators, the truth to compare against, and the **effective sample size**. Weighted IS lands within a hundredth of the truth, with an ESS in the thousands.

Now the honest failure: **deploy the DP-optimal policy**, a large change from what was logged.

The effective sample size collapses to a few dozen, and the app **flags the estimate unreliable** instead of quoting it. A few episodes carry all the weight — that is the variance problem, not a bug.

Coverage is checked too. If the target policy would take an action the logged policy never takes, the estimate is refused outright.

## 16. Closing cards

**How to run it**
- **pip install -r requirements.txt**
- **uvicorn backend_app:app --port 8000** → open http://localhost:8000/
- No server? Open **clutchiq.html** — every RL screen still works
- **pytest -q** → 130 tests, including Python-to-JavaScript parity
- **python rl_experiments.py** → regenerates every number in the app

**What is real, and what is not — The line to hold in a viva**
- **Real:** uploaded histories → features, clusters, matchmaking, burnout, MC prediction
- **Simulated:** player reactions to difficulty, every RL episode, bandit rewards
- **Model estimate:** the transition probabilities, from 144,000 rollouts
- **Proxy:** rage quits and closeness when the data does not log them
- The policy is learned on a documented simulator — not fitted to real players
